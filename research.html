Research

Heat, sleep and physiological strain in free-living populations

My research asks a deceptively simple question: how much heat is too much, and for whom? Answering it means measuring people where they actually live and sleep, modelling exposure–response relationships that are rarely linear, and then turning those relationships into thresholds that a public health agency can act on.

Three strands run through the work — field measurement, statistical modelling, and evidence synthesis — and most projects use all three.

Project HEATS — Heat Exposure, Activity and Sleep

Analytical lead · NRF CREATE programme · 2025–present

Project HEATS characterises the indoor thermal environment of Singapore homes and dormitories and links it to objectively measured sleep. I lead the statistical analysis and manuscript development for the heat exposure workstream.

Design. Wearable and in-home monitoring across three field waves (Jun 2025 – Jul 2026): bedroom temperature, humidity, CO₂ and particulates alongside actigraphy-derived sleep outcomes. The primary analysis covers 122 participants and 10,303 person-nights.

Methods. Linear mixed-effects models with Mundlak within/between decomposition, which lets us separate what a person does in response to a hot night (behavioural adaptation) from what heat does to them (physiological strain) — a distinction that a single pooled coefficient hides. Non-linear threshold work uses piecewise and segmented models with AIC-based knot search and restricted cubic splines, comparing dry-bulb temperature against composite indices (wet-bulb temperature, Heat Index) as predictors of sleep disruption.

Outputs. A protocol paper under review at BMC Public Health; the primary sleep-continuity analysis in preparation; three oral presentations at Indoor Air 2026; a 30-night field trial of a dynamic “down-rise” bedroom cooling profile.

Findings from this programme were covered nationally in The Straits Times and Lianhe Zaobao in June 2026, and cited by the Chairman of the National Research Foundation Singapore.

Exertional Heat Vulnerability Index (eHVI)

Evidence-synthesis lead · protocol lead and guarantor · NRF & NEA, CISR-2024-2R-04

A Climate Impact Science Research programme building a heat vulnerability index for Singapore. I lead the evidence-synthesis workstream: I developed the systematic review protocol underpinning the index — search strategy, extraction framework and synthesis approach — and serve as guarantor of the review. I also chair the bi-weekly technical review and act as quality gate for the report blocks delivered to the funder.

Registered as PROSPERO CRD420261289729.

Cooling Singapore 2.0

Analytical lead, physiological workstream · NRF

Controlled laboratory heat-exposure studies across population groups who are usually treated as a single “general public” in advisory design: children, heat-unacclimatised adults and older adults. I model the physiological and perceptual responses and translate them into policy-relevant insight for tailoring national heat-health advisories — the point being that one threshold for everyone protects nobody particularly well.

Evidence synthesis

Four PROSPERO-registered reviews, two of which I lead and two of which I support methodologically. Together they map what is actually known about nighttime heat and about the tools used to warn people.

Behaviour, nutrition and health

My doctoral and postdoctoral work addressed a parallel question in a different domain: what determines what people actually eat, and which of those determinants can be moved?

  • Team sport athletes (PhD, TUS Athlone) — dietary intake against sports nutrition guidelines, nutrition knowledge, and cooking and food skills, using a mixed-methods design to identify modifiable behavioural targets.
  • UK households in the cost-of-living crisis (King’s College London) — cooking and food behaviours, food insecurity, diet quality and mental wellbeing, combining hierarchical regression on large survey datasets with thematic analysis of focus group and open-text data.

The methodological thread — behaviour is a modifiable exposure, and you have to measure it well before you can change it — is the same one that runs through the heat work.
